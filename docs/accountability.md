# What to do when you have crossed someone's boundaries

Based on [guidance by Misha Bonaventura](https://docs.google.com/document/d/15Y_HVW0gSc67sWam_I2xMJmZBcX2izbleg1k_qf5n0Y/edit#heading=h.8mm68tlmftnh), edited by Marc.

> It is often not the act that creates a falling out, but the reaction to the accusation that makes someone seem unaccountable and makes repair difficult or unattainable. The difference between someone who is dangerous and someone who made a mistake is their ability and willingness to take responsibility for their mistakes and make changes to their behaviour. Humility is your best friend right now.

## First, what not to do

- Do not defend yourself or make excuses. Trust that you will be able to tell your story after you have fully heard the person you hurt.
- Do not blame the person telling you they have been harmed by you.
- Do not keep talking when things are going poorly.
- Do not ghost the person.
- Do not gaslight the person. If you do not know what this is, look it up.
- Do not say anything resembling, “Yeah, but…”

Read [So your friend got called out](https://medium.com/@SadistHailey/so-your-friend-got-called-out-fc2746a52f85), which can also help the people supporting you right now.

## Step 1: Understand consent

Consent is:

- **Competent.** Everyone involved has the necessary ability, knowledge or skill to have a sexual interaction.
- **Informed.** Everyone involved can make a decision based on an understanding of the facts of the situation.
- **Unpressured.** Nobody uses persuasion, influence or intimidation to make someone do something.
- **Specific.** Everyone is clear about what they are doing together.
- **Ongoing.** Communication continues throughout the interaction.
- **Affirmative.** Agreement to a statement or request is expressed.

This definition comes from the [San Francisco Sex Information](http://sfsi.org/) sex education class.

## Step 2: Understand how you might have violated consent

- **Safety.** Physical safety can be in question when one person is physically stronger, or when someone is in an unfamiliar place or situation.
- **Sobriety.** Drugs or alcohol on either side can make it difficult or impossible to ask for or give consent.
- **Persuasion.** Pushing for what you want without paying attention to the other person's needs. Asking once is fine, asking twice is checking, and asking a third time is harassment.
- **Assumptions.** Overstepping what was agreed to in an interaction. This often happens when your desire overrides your awareness of the other person.
- **Power dynamics.** Do you have employment, social or educational power over them? If so, slow down.
- **Withholding information.** Do they know everything they need to know to make an informed choice about being with you?

## Step 3: What happened?

You can have your experience, just as the other person can have theirs. That does not mean you did not hurt them.

Recognise how frightening it can be to be accused of hurting someone, especially someone you care about. If you are in a calm conversation but are struggling not to react, you can say something like:

> Can I have a moment to take in what you are telling me? I want to be fully present with you, but I am noticing that I have a lot of emotion inside and want to defend myself. I know that is not what you need right now, and I need a minute.

Take a moment to help your nervous system calm down. Take a few deep breaths, put your hand on your chest, close your eyes and notice what is happening inside your body.

When you are ready, listen to what happened for them and be open to seeing where you made a mistake. Be curious about where things went wrong for them. Most of all, they need you to listen and hear what happened for them.

## Step 4: What you can do

### Listen and do not make excuses

It has probably been hard enough for the other person to find the courage to tell you. Do not make the experience any harder. If it really was a consent accident or misunderstanding, they will be much more likely to listen to your experience if you first listen to theirs and show concern.

If you were driving a car, made a serious mistake and caused an accident, you would be sorry for the passengers in your car. If you were guiding a sexual experience and made a serious mistake that took the experience off track for someone, be sorry and do what you can to be present with their experience.

Do not try to justify your actions as your first response. From your perspective, it may have seemed as though they were comfortable throughout the interaction. They were not. Do not tell them that they were. Your experience and their experience can be completely different. Validate their experience by listening to them.

You may find [The Importance of Empathy in Everyday Life](https://lifehacker.com/the-importance-of-empathy-in-everyday-life-1791961488) helpful.

### Ask what you can do to make it better

- They might not know immediately. Give them time to think about it.
- What you hear may not be easy. You might be challenged to do some of the hardest work of your life, but be willing to hear it and learn from the experience.
- If the interaction involved drugs or alcohol, you might need to stop using them or get help with stopping.
- If you were not attentive to the other person in the moment, consider somatic-based therapy.
- If the person never wants to see you again, give them space. You can still do the work to change without involving them. Create an accountability pod and begin an accountability process.

### Form an accountability pod

The person you hurt may not be able or willing to confront you or talk to you about what happened. You can still do the work to make sure it does not happen again.

Ask a group of people to help hold you accountable for doing better. Ask them for feedback and talk about the issues connected to the situation. Choose people from your community who know you well, and at least one person who does not. They should care about you and show up for you, while also being willing to challenge you honestly.

Check whether you currently have enough time and stability for this process. Do not make your pod work hard for you. They are providing a service, so do everything you can to keep the emotional and practical labour off them. Be patient: they have their own lives too.

### Topics to consider

- Read articles about consent and accountability. Start with [Misha's consent research](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1gxYxTrmDSZ7_y0GPMhWZ832y_IukrlLpqmIormJFqd8/edit?usp=sharing) and the [resources below](#resources), then discuss what you learn with your pod.
- Stop using drugs or alcohol, especially in sexual situations. Consider AA or NA and getting a sponsor. [Find AA meetings](https://www.alcoholics-anonymous-berlin.de/our-meetings).
- Join self-help groups or go to therapy. Therapy is considered indispensable in many accountability processes. Read [How I Found a Therapist for My Accountability Process](https://docs.google.com/document/d/19bG_rIUiCE5BOuLNFVXSNaNT5Zwru_ONX9qVQHnY3DY/edit?usp=sharing).
- Look at documentation from other accountability processes, such as [Reid Mihalko's accountability process](https://medium.com/reid-mihalkos-accountability-process).

### Make intentions and agreements

How will you know you are making progress? Set out some intentions and agreements at the beginning. Along the way, your pod and other interested people can give you feedback.

Clarify what accountability means in relation to:

- yourself;
- the people whose boundaries you crossed;
- your accountability pod; and
- the community.

### Record your journey and lessons

- Use a document to track your process. You can use this [example of tracking accountability measures](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1IIEjAuUMxrnKiAa3bxJrL_Rj5SmcGXBO7-V2WVfd26A/edit?usp=sharing) as a structure.
- Record the work as you do it so you can report back to interested people.
- Do the work yourself and take as much labour as possible off other people. Make it easy for anyone concerned to understand your journey and lessons.
- Summarise what you have learned so you can share it with others.
- You may never get back what you had, but you can make sure you do not repeat the same mistakes. Documenting your journey can help you respond to future concerns about your behaviour.
